# PJ2 – Bowtie Key

DE: Schwalbenschwanz-Klammer · JP: chigiri (契り)

A double-dovetail key inlaid across a split. Wide at both ends and pinched at
the waist, so it cannot withdraw in the plane of the face: pulling the two
sides of the crack apart drives the key's flared ends into sound timber.

`kind: "patch"` — bounded in every direction, shallow, and open only at the
face it is let into.

## Geometry

One curve cutter, extruded down from the top face:

| | canonical | of the section |
|---|---|---|
| length along the beam | 1.10 | 1.1 × |
| width at the ends | ±0.34 | 68% |
| width at the waist | ±0.13 | 26% |
| inlay depth | 0.22 | 22% |

Waist is 38% of the end width, which is the usual proportion — narrower and
the key is fragile across its own short grain, wider and it stops locking.

Kept fraction 0.963. Verified against the model: removed width is ±0.313 at
the ends and ±0.130 at the waist, depth z +0.280 to +0.499 against a top face
at +0.5.

## Orientation matters, and it is the thing to get right

The key must cross the split. A shake or check in a beam runs **along** the
grain, so the key's long axis has to run **across** it. As authored here the
long axis lies along the beam, which suits a transverse joint line — two
members butted end to end, or a crack running across the face.

For a longitudinal shake, rotate the placement 90°. The kernel's `rotation`
input does this; it does not need a second catalogue entry.

## Purpose

Stops a split propagating. It does not close a split, does not restore lost
section, and is not a repair for decay — use PJ1 for missing material and a
splice for lost capacity. What it does is arrest a crack cheaply, visibly and
reversibly, which is often exactly what a heritage repair calls for.

## Structural behaviour

Carries tension **across** the split, in the plane of the face, and nothing
else. Depth is shallow by design, so it engages only the outer fibres — two
keys on opposite faces are usual where the split runs through.

Insertion is straight down into the face and nothing else: the flare blocks
every direction in the plane. That makes it one of the few entries whose
insertion cone is genuinely a single ray, alongside SJ7 and SJ8.

## Fabrication

1. Lay the key on the face, spanning the split, and scribe around it.
2. Saw or rout the four flared flanks, staying outside the line.
3. Waste out and pare the floor flat — the key must bed on its whole area.
4. Drive the key in from the face; it should need a mallet.
5. Plane flush once the glue has set.

Tools: marking knife, fine saw or router, paring chisel, mallet.

## Effort

Low, but unforgiving: the flanks are short, cross-grain and visible, and an
over-cut flank cannot be recovered. Cut the key first and scribe the pocket
from it, never the other way round.

## Provenance

Authored in `gh_joint_author`. An earlier draft (`PT_1.json`) had the same
outline at roughly six times the size — the waist fell at ±0.628 against a
section half-width of 0.5, so the pinch happened outside the timber and the
cut removed the whole upper half at every station. It passed the splice check
by sampling noise (kept share 0.507 against 0.490). Worth remembering: a
patch profile has to be checked against the section, not just drawn.
