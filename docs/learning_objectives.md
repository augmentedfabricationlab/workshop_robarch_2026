At workshop completion, participants will be able to:

- **Turn heterogeneous observations into a structured model.** Capture text, images, and scans of an existing structure and translate them into a graph-based Assembly Model of components, connections, and damage states.
- **Prompt for action, not description.** Formulate structured prompts with defined roles, repair goals, and constraints (tools, materials, robotic reach and capability) that yield an Action Model rather than prose.
- **Generate and compare repair strategies.** Produce more than one procedural repair plan for the same damage and make the successive intervention steps explicit enough to be debated.
- **Plan and execute robot-enabled actions.** Set up and run scanning, marking, stabilisation, and milling operations, accounting for kinematics, end-effectors, and workspace limits.
- **Close the loop through measurement.** Apply the scan-after-cut cycle to verify fit, and use the discrepancy between model and material as design information.
- **Allocate work by comparative advantage.** Decide which steps belong to the robot and which to the hand, and define the safety and feedback loops that make in-situ collaboration workable.
- **Locate human judgment in an AI-supported workflow.** Identify the points, framing, verification, comparison, evaluation, where domain expertise remains essential.

---

## Where humans stay in the loop

The workflow is built to *propose*, not to decide.

The largest gap in this kind of pipeline is between idealised prismatic geometry and real, scanned timber: warped, weathered, out of square, with a history. Closing that gap is the substance of the workshop, and it is closed by looking, measuring, and judging, not by the model.
